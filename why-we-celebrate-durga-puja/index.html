Why do we celebrate Durga Puja?

The story begins with Maa Durga's victory over Mahishasura. Its meaning grows through homecoming, worship, art, food and the experience of building a temporary home together.

The short answer

Durga Puja celebrates Goddess Durga's victory over Mahishasura and the triumph of Shakti over destructive forces. In Bengali tradition, it also welcomes Durga as a daughter returning to her maternal home with Lakshmi, Saraswati, Kartik and Ganesha.

That is why the festival can feel devotional, celebratory and emotional at the same time. It is worship, but it is also family, art, music, food, friendship and community.

The story of Durga and Mahishasura

One of the central stories of Durga Puja comes from the Devi Mahatmya. Mahishasura, often described as a powerful buffalo demon, becomes a force the gods cannot defeat on their own. Goddess Durga confronts him and ultimately wins.

The victory is commonly understood as good overcoming evil, but the story also centres Shakti, divine feminine power. Durga is not presented as a passive figure. She is strength, protection, intelligence and decisive action brought together.

For many devotees, this is the spiritual centre of the Puja: destructive power may appear overwhelming, but it is not beyond resistance.

Why Maa Durga is also a daughter coming home

The sacred story has a deeply personal Bengali expression. Durga is welcomed not only as a goddess, but also as a daughter returning to her maternal home with her children, Lakshmi, Saraswati, Kartik and Ganesha.

This changes the emotional texture of the festival. Before she arrives there is anticipation. While she is present there is celebration. On Dashami there is sadness because the visit ends. The familiar promise is that she will return again.

For people living away from where they grew up, this idea of homecoming can become even more meaningful. A community Puja creates a temporary home where familiar sounds, food, language and rituals return for a few days.

Why Durga Puja is more than a single ritual

DevotionPuja, Pushpanjali, Arati and other observances give the celebration its religious centre.
CommunityPeople organise, volunteer, welcome visitors, share Bhog and spend time together across several days.
CultureMusic, dance, storytelling and performance help each community express its own history and identity.
HomecomingThe image of Durga visiting her maternal home turns a cosmic story into something recognisable and intimate.

The exact customs, ritual practices and cultural programmes can differ between communities. That variation is part of why Durga Puja can feel local and personal even when the central story is shared.

What Mahalaya and Bijoya add to the story

Mahalaya is associated with the beginning of Devi Paksha and the invocation of the Goddess in popular Bengali tradition. The mood changes before the main Puja days begin. For many families, hearing Chandipath in the early morning marks the emotional beginning of Pujo.

Bijoya Dashami brings the farewell. Yet Bijoya is not only an ending. It is also a time for pronam to elders, greetings, embraces, sweets and reconnecting. The departure of the Goddess leads back into human relationships.
